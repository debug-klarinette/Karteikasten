# Fabrikplanung · VL 03 – Location planning

### Which combination of characteristics makes location decisions especially risky?
- [ ] Short planning horizon, easy reversibility and low investment volume
- [x] Long-term planning horizon, irreversibility, high investment volume and high uncertainty in location-specific factors
- [ ] Purely operational scope with monthly adjustment cycles
- [ ] Low entrepreneurial risk because only monetary factors are involved
> Explanation: Location decisions have the longest horizon of all factory-planning phases (~15–30 years), are largely irreversible, tie up high investment volumes and depend on hard-to-predict location factors. This combination creates high overall entrepreneurial risk, so they must be planned with utmost precision.

### A carmaker builds a plant abroad mainly to enter a new sales market, avoiding tariffs and transport costs and building up local content. Which location strategy is this?
- [x] Market development
- [ ] Cost reduction
- [ ] Proximity to key customers
- [ ] Exploiting technology and knowledge
> Explanation: The four strategies are market development, cost reduction, proximity to key customers, and exploiting technology/knowledge. Producing locally to open a market, avoid trade barriers and generate local content is market development — a less intense form being CKD (Completely Knocked Down) production.

### Why do companies use Completely Knocked Down (CKD) production?
- [ ] Because fully assembled products enjoy the lowest import tariffs
- [x] Because partially or completely disassembled products (kits) are subject to significantly lower taxes, and local assembly adds local content in the importing country
- [ ] Because it eliminates the need for any local workforce
- [ ] Because it removes all coordination costs between locations
> Explanation: In CKD, kits rather than complete products are exported; partially/fully disassembled goods are taxed much lower, and the local assembly contributes to value creation in the importing country (local content). It is a lighter form of the market-development strategy.

### Which implementation form of a location strategy is correctly described?
- [ ] Consolidation = redistributing production from one site to several sites
- [x] Consolidation = concentrating the production of two or more locations at a single site
- [ ] Relocation = keeping all locations but reassigning processes between them
- [ ] Allocation = completely shutting down one location and resuming production elsewhere
> Explanation: Relocation = full shutdown and restart elsewhere; consolidation = concentrating 2+ locations at one site; allocation = redistributing activities from one site to several; partial relocation = same number of sites but reassigned processes. A location selection is only required for establishment or expansion.

### According to Kinkel, into which three categories can location factors be classified?
- [ ] Global, regional and local factors
- [x] Market, production and performance factors
- [ ] Firm, minimum and desired factors
- [ ] Monetary, non-monetary and dynamic factors
> Explanation: Location factors are grouped into market, production and performance factors — showing that a location's performance level cannot be judged by production factors alone. (Global/regional/local is a separate categorization by level; monetary-quantitative / non-monetary-quantitative / qualitative is the sub-level distinction.)

### What is the difference between location factors and location criteria?
- [ ] They are synonyms and are used interchangeably
- [x] Location factors describe the characteristics of a location; location criteria are the company's requirements for a location, derived from its goals and summarized in a requirements profile
- [ ] Location factors are always qualitative; location criteria are always monetary
- [ ] Location criteria describe the location; location factors describe the company's needs
> Explanation: Location factors comprehensively characterize a site. Location criteria express what the company requires of a site (derived from company goals, structured like the factors into a requirements profile), against which alternatives are compared.

### Which of the three types of location requirements acts as a knock-out (must-meet) criterion?
- [x] Firm requirements — every location must meet them, otherwise it is unsuitable
- [ ] Desired requirements — they decide only between otherwise equal alternatives
- [ ] Minimum requirements — they only enhance an already suitable location
- [ ] Dynamic requirements — they change over time and are therefore ignored
> Explanation: Firm requirements are knock-out criteria; failing them makes a location unsuitable. Minimum requirements define a threshold whose violation is equally disqualifying. Desired requirements are not mandatory but become decisive when several alternatives meet firm and minimum requirements equally.

### In the rough (initial) selection, when is a location alternative kept as "suitable"?
- [ ] When it meets at least one desired requirement
- [x] Only when it meets all firm and all minimum requirements
- [ ] When it has the lowest labor costs among all alternatives
- [ ] When it maximizes the number of desired requirements, regardless of firm ones
> Explanation: The rough selection compares the requirements profile with each location's characteristics profile; only alternatives meeting all firm and minimum requirements pass to the detailed evaluation. Note that factors are dynamic — a threshold met today may be exceeded in later years.

### Why can a simple comparison of hourly labor costs between countries be misleading for location selection?
- [ ] Because labor costs never change over time
- [x] Because workers differ in qualification, productivity and work quality, and relocating/operating a foreign site causes additional costs
- [ ] Because labor costs are the only relevant location factor
- [ ] Because low-wage countries always deliver higher product quality
> Explanation: Wages are a dynamic factor and must not be viewed in isolation. Countries differ in qualification, productivity and quality of the workforce, and foreign operation adds coordination and other costs — so a pure wage comparison can mislead.

### Which method/index is used to assess the qualitative benefit (country risk) of a location alternative?
- [ ] Net present value (NPV)
- [x] Country ratings such as the BERI index (Business Environment Risk Information)
- [ ] Payback period
- [ ] ABC analysis
> Explanation: Quantitative evaluation typically uses investment-appraisal methods like NPV. Qualitative benefit is assessed via country ratings; the best-known is the BERI index, a leaderboard of investment attractiveness capturing risks such as transfer and expropriation risk. Uncertainty is handled via sensitivity analysis, scenario technique and event-tree analysis.

### In the BMW Debrecen location decision, how did the selection process narrow down the alternatives?
- [ ] A single site was fixed in advance for political reasons
- [x] Around 170 potential Central European sites were screened and narrowed to three final candidates (Debrecen, Miskolc, Košice) by matching required criteria against available factors
- [ ] All 170 sites were built simultaneously to compare them empirically
- [ ] Only labor cost was considered, ignoring subsidies and personnel availability
> Explanation: With a consulting firm, ~170 sites were screened, then reduced to three finalists (Debrecen and Miskolc in Hungary, Košice in Slovakia) by comparing required location criteria with available factors. Debrecen won on cost (~1/5 lower manufacturing cost, aided by state subsidies), qualified personnel, supplier co-location (CATL batteries), network integration and renewable-energy sustainability.

### What is meant by the "decomposition of value creation" in globalized value networks?
- [ ] Concentrating all value creation at the company's headquarters
- [x] Distributing value-added steps internationally across several locations, each performing activities that match its location profile
- [ ] Abandoning international production in favor of pure export from the home site
- [ ] Producing every component redundantly at every location
> Explanation: A location is no longer isolated but a link in a global value chain. Value creation is distributed internationally, each site contributing according to its profile — the decomposition of value creation, enabled from ~1980 by cheaper communication/transport, deregulation and customs unions.

### For a resilient, cost-efficient value network (Sartorius/machine-tool logic), how should assemblies and competencies be allocated? (Mehrfachauswahl)
- [x] Simple, low-know-how assemblies (e.g. frame, base plate, housing) are produced at low-cost locations
- [x] Complex, know-how-intensive assemblies and core competencies are kept/developed at high-tech locations or the domestic headquarters
- [x] Variant creation is done late and close to the customer, keeping modules variant-neutral and highly standardized for as long as possible
- [ ] Core competencies should be freely spread to all low-cost locations to maximize knowledge exchange
> Explanation: Assemblies are classified by required know-how: simple ones go to low-cost sites, complex ones to high-tech sites. Core competencies stay at HQ to protect against knowledge loss. High standardization plus late, customer-close variant creation (as at Sartorius with its standardized monolithic weighing block) keeps costs low while meeting customer needs.
