# Fabrikplanung · VL 05 – Manufacturing and Assembly System Planning

### How are "manufacturing" and "assembly" distinguished as subsystems of a production system?
- [ ] Manufacturing joins components; assembly produces parts with defined shapes
- [x] Manufacturing produces parts with a geometrically defined shape; assembly joins components/assemblies into products of greater complexity
- [ ] Both perform exactly the same tasks and are interchangeable terms
- [ ] Manufacturing only handles information flow; assembly only handles energy flow
> Explanation: A production system (input–output–work task) splits by task into manufacturing and assembly. Manufacturing produces geometrically defined parts (shaping/altering workpieces); assembly joins parts and assemblies into higher-complexity products, supported by material-flow and packaging technology.

### According to DIN 8580, what is the PRIMARY task of manufacturing, and which are secondary tasks?
- [x] Primary: altering shape/material properties (primary shaping, transforming, separating, coating, changing material properties); secondary: checking, transporting, handling, storing
- [ ] Primary: transporting and storing; secondary: shaping and separating
- [ ] Primary: joining workpieces; secondary: primary shaping
- [ ] Primary: quality inspection; secondary: transforming and coating
> Explanation: DIN 8580 defines part manufacturing as the stepwise change of a workpiece's shape and/or material properties (primary shaping, transforming, separating, coating, changing material properties). Secondary tasks (checking, transporting, handling, storing per VDI 2860) support the main task and also appear in other production areas.

### Why is assembly called the "catchment area for all errors," and what dominates its time budget?
- [x] Because it sits at the end of the process so all earlier design/manufacturing errors converge there; joining is the only value-adding step but takes only a small share of time, dominated by secondary activities (positioning, handling)
- [ ] Because joining accounts for nearly all of the assembly time
- [ ] Because assembly generates the most scrap material of all processes
- [ ] Because it is the first step and defines all downstream tolerances
> Explanation: Assembly joins pre-manufactured parts into a functional product and lies at the process end, so upstream errors (poor design, inferior parts) surface there. Joining is the actual value-adding activity but consumes little time; secondary activities (bringing/positioning joining partners, handling tools) dominate and should be minimized. Hence: assembly-friendly design and error-tolerant systems.

### What distinguishes flexibility from adaptability (Wandlungsfähigkeit) in production systems?
- [x] Flexibility handles anticipated changes within a predetermined corridor (fast, without investment); adaptability handles changes beyond the corridor / in new dimensions (fast, with low investment)
- [ ] Flexibility handles unforeseen changes; adaptability only anticipated ones
- [ ] They are identical; both require large investments
- [ ] Flexibility concerns only quality; adaptability only cost
> Explanation: Changeability is the umbrella term. Flexibility covers anticipated changes inside a known corridor quickly and essentially without investment; adaptability is the potential to act beyond that corridor and in new dimensions, quickly and with low investment — necessary in turbulent markets, but higher adaptability generally raises investment cost.

### Match the flexibility types to their meaning. Which pairings are correct? (Mehrfachauswahl)
- [x] Process flexibility ↔ redundancy: a workpiece group can be made on different systems, so one can switch if a machine fails
- [x] Product flexibility ↔ producing orders from the known product range in any sequence
- [x] Volume flexibility ↔ increasing/decreasing production capacity within technical/economic limits
- [ ] Expansion flexibility ↔ the inability to add any further equipment to the system
> Explanation: Process flexibility brings redundancy (alternative systems on failure); product flexibility means any-sequence production of the known range; volume flexibility scales capacity up/down; expansion flexibility is the ability to enhance the system by integrating additional processing/material-flow/information facilities (adaptation flexibility handles production-program changes).

### In the division of labor, what characterizes "division by quantity" (centralization by object)?
- [ ] Similar tasks are grouped so each station performs only one operation type on many products
- [x] Different tasks on similar products are grouped so each station completes the whole task; every station needs all tools/equipment and trained workers — limited to low quantities/less complex tasks
- [ ] It is only applicable to fully automated transfer lines for mass production
- [ ] It forbids assigning any variant to more than one station
> Explanation: Division by quantity (centralization by object) distributes units evenly across complete workstations/universal machining centers; each must hold all tools and skills, so it suits low quantities and simpler tasks. Division by type (centralization of operations) instead groups similar operations into stations. Hybrid forms combine both.

### Which statement about production types is correct?
- [x] Single-unit production splits into one-off and repeated production; mass customization is a hybrid combining mass production with customer-individual products
- [ ] Series production means each product is manufactured only once to customer order
- [ ] Mass production is characterized by very low initial cost and high cost per unit
- [ ] Single-unit and mass production can never be combined in any way
> Explanation: Types are single-unit (one-off vs repeated), series (small/medium/large) and mass production. Mass production has very high initial cost but low unit cost with frequent process repetition. ICT increasingly enables hybrids such as mass customization — products individualized to customer preference at series-like efficiency.

### Match the production principle (spatial structure) to its description. Which pairings are correct? (Mehrfachauswahl)
- [x] Site production ↔ the product stays in a fixed location and components are brought to it (e.g. large machine tools)
- [x] Workshop production ↔ facilities of the same type are grouped; workpieces move in batches between stations
- [x] Flow production ↔ resources are arranged in the process sequence; highest form of product orientation
- [ ] Group production ↔ a single machine performs the entire product with no grouping of stations
> Explanation: Site (Baustellen) production keeps the product fixed; workshop (Werkstatt) production groups like machines and moves batches; group production spatially clusters different-type stations for related sub-processes; flow production arranges resources along the process sequence (highest product orientation). Group production is a cluster of different stations, not a single machine.

### Which advantages/disadvantages correctly characterize flow production versus workshop (job-shop) production?
- [x] Flow: clear material flow, short throughput times, low stock — but limited flexibility and high changeover cost. Workshop: high flexibility and fault redundancy — but poor production transparency and long transport routes
- [ ] Flow production offers the highest flexibility to product-range changes
- [ ] Workshop production guarantees the shortest throughput times and lowest stock
- [ ] Flow production requires the longest transport routes of all principles
> Explanation: Flow production gives a clear material flow, short throughput and little stock, at the cost of low flexibility, high changeover cost and often special machines. Workshop production is highly flexible with redundancy but suffers from low transparency, long transport routes and high work-preparation effort.

### As variant count/quantity and degree of automation change, how are manufacturing systems classified, and what is "scalability"?
- [x] From high-variant/low-automation to low-variant/high-automation: unlinked NC manufacturing, manufacturing island, machining center, FMS, flexible manufacturing line, transfer line; scalability = later raising the automation level (e.g. upgrading an NC machine with handling/monitoring to a machining center)
- [ ] Transfer lines suit high-variant, low-volume prototyping
- [ ] Unlinked NC manufacturing is the most automated, rigidly linked system
- [ ] Scalability means reducing the product range a system can handle
> Explanation: Low quantity → high order-change frequency and low automation (unlinked NC, islands); low variety → higher automation (FMS, flexible/transfer lines). Scalability is the ability to later increase automation, e.g. adding handling and monitoring to a standalone NC machine to form a machining center, then linking centers into an FMS.

### How does a "classic" transfer line differ from a Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS)?
- [x] The transfer line is rigidly linked and specialized for a fixed product range (large series/mass), convertible only with high effort; an FMS links machines flexibly via automated workpiece/tool/information flow and processes different workpieces in any order
- [ ] The transfer line processes any workpiece in any order with no changeover effort
- [ ] The FMS is a single-machine system with no material-flow linkage
- [ ] Both are identical; only the name differs
> Explanation: An FMS combines flexible manufacturing cells with automated workpiece/tool/information flows for multi-product, multi-stage machining in variable sequence. A transfer line is fixed-linked, flow-organized and specialized on one product range, so it is efficient for large series/mass but costly to convert.

### The four basic assembly-system types are chosen by certainty/size of production volume and certainty/duration of product lifespan. Which pairing is correct?
- [x] Uncertain volume + uncertain lifespan → manual assembly system; certain (high) volume + certain lifespan → fully automated assembly system
- [ ] Uncertain volume + uncertain lifespan → fully automated assembly system
- [ ] Certain high volume + certain lifespan → manual assembly system
- [ ] The four types are separated by fixed universal unit numbers valid for every product
> Explanation: Manual systems fit uncertain volume and uncertain lifespan; adaptive systems fit uncertain volume but certain lifespan; flexibly automated cells fit certain volume but uncertain lifespan; fully automated systems fit certain high volume and certain lifespan. The dividing thresholds (≈100,000–1,000,000 units/yr; 6 months–3 years) are product-specific, not universal.

### A machine group processes 12,000 units/year, processing time 5 min/unit, batch size 1,000 units, set-up 6 h/batch. What is the annual machine occupancy time (Tp + Ts)?
- [x] ≈ 1072 h (Tp = 1000 h + Ts = 72 h)
- [ ] ≈ 1000 h (processing time only, set-up ignored)
- [ ] ≈ 1006 h (only one set-up per year)
- [ ] ≈ 60,072 h (minutes not converted to hours)
> Explanation: Tp = quantity × processing time = 12,000 × 5 min = 60,000 min = 1000 h. Batches/year = 12,000 ÷ 1,000 = 12, so Ts = 12 × 6 h = 72 h. Machine occupancy = Tp + Ts = 1000 + 72 = 1072 h. Dividing occupancy by available capacity per machine gives the required number of machines.

### Daily demand is n = 400 units, assembly time te = 6 min/unit. A worker attends ta = 480 min/day with tb = 30 min breaks (assume utilization Su = 100 %, performance level PL = 100 %). How many workstations m are required (m = C/C')?
- [x] 6 workstations (C = 2400 min/day, C' = 450 min/day, m = 5.33 → round up)
- [ ] 5 workstations (5.33 rounded down)
- [ ] 4 workstations
- [ ] 8 workstations
> Explanation: Capacity demand C = n·te / Su = 400 × 6 / 1 = 2400 min/day. Capacity supply per worker C' = (ta − tb)·PL = (480 − 30) × 1 = 450 min/day. m = C / C' = 2400 / 450 = 5.33; since fractional workstations are impossible, round up to 6.

### In equipment procurement (VDI/VDE 3694), how do the specification sheet (Lastenheft) and the functional specification (Pflichtenheft) differ?
- [x] The Lastenheft compiles all customer requirements (the "what") and is the basis for the tender; the Pflichtenheft is the contractor's description of how/with what those requirements will be realized and requires client approval
- [ ] The Pflichtenheft lists the customer's requirements; the Lastenheft is the supplier's solution
- [ ] Both are written solely by the customer with no supplier involvement
- [ ] Neither has any legal relevance for the project
> Explanation: Per VDI/VDE 3694, the Lastenheft (specification sheet) compiles all customer requirements/scope and serves as the tender basis. The Pflichtenheft (functional specification) describes the contractor's realization ("how" and "with what"); after client approval it becomes, with company guidelines, the binding agreement for both parties.
