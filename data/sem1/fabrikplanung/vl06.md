# Fabrikplanung · VL 06 – Logistics Planning

### The three main logistics objectives are performance, cost and quality. Which conflict of objectives does the lecture emphasize?
- [x] Automation vs. flexibility, and high utilization vs. short lead time (high utilization raises waiting times and thus lead times)
- [ ] Performance and cost can always be maximized together without any trade-off
- [ ] Quality and on-time delivery are mutually exclusive
- [ ] Automation always increases flexibility at no cost
> Explanation: The components of performance, cost and quality cannot all be maximized simultaneously. Automating material flow boosts performance/reliability but reduces flexibility; and driving utilization high (to save space/capital) lengthens waiting and hence lead times. Logistics planning seeks the overall optimum across these conflicts.

### What are the "7 R" (seven rights) of logistics?
- [x] The right product, in the right quantity, at the right time, in the right quality, at the right place, at the right price, with the right information
- [ ] The right robot, route, rack, ramp, region, rate and report
- [ ] Reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, refuse, repair, rethink
- [ ] Receiving, replenishment, retrieval, routing, roll-out, return, review
> Explanation: Intra-company logistics aims to provide the right product, quantity, time, quality, place, price and information. This encapsulates the task of supplying production with the needed materials at lowest possible cost.

### Order the five production strategies by customer integration / lead time and match an example. Which statements are correct? (Mehrfachauswahl)
- [x] Engineer-to-order has the highest customer integration and longest lead time (e.g. plant construction)
- [x] Make-to-stock has the lowest customer integration and shortest lead time (e.g. consumer goods)
- [x] Assemble-to-order means order-specific assembly from pre-produced/stored components (e.g. cars, computers)
- [ ] Configure-to-order develops a brand-new product from scratch per customer order
> Explanation: Lead time and customer integration decrease from Engineer-to-order → Make-to-order → Assemble-to-order → Configure-to-order → Make-to-stock. Configure-to-order takes a fully manufactured product and only configures it to the order (e.g. a power supply set for EU/US), so it does not develop a new product.

### What is the Order Penetration Point (OPP), and what is a goal of Supply Chain Design regarding it?
- [x] The OPP is the transition between order-neutral (push/forecast-driven) and order-specific (pull/order-driven) production; a goal is to shift it as far toward the customer as possible to save inventory and shorten response time
- [ ] The OPP is the point of highest inventory that should be pushed upstream to the raw-material supplier
- [ ] The OPP marks where forecast-driven production begins and should be moved away from the customer
- [ ] The OPP is irrelevant once a production strategy is chosen
> Explanation: The OPP is where product specs freeze and the last stock is held — separating the forecast-driven (push, make-to-stock) part from the order-driven (pull, make/assemble-to-order) part. Shifting it toward the customer yields make/assemble-to-order behaviour, saving inventory and usually shortening response time. Bottleneck resources, however, are best kept upstream of the OPP (run make-to-stock).

### How do single sourcing and multiple sourcing differ, and when is single sourcing justified?
- [x] Single sourcing uses one supplier per part (low interchangeability); multiple sourcing uses several redundant suppliers (high interchangeability). Single sourcing is justified e.g. when the partner holds key development competence or tooling costs are very high
- [ ] Single sourcing always maximizes supply security and should be the default
- [ ] Multiple sourcing means one supplier who serves multiple plants
- [ ] Multiple sourcing reduces the number of supplier relationships to a minimum
> Explanation: Sourcing strategies range from single (one supplier/part) to multiple (redundant suppliers/part). For supply security several suppliers are usually contracted, but fewer suppliers per part (e.g. dual sourcing) reduce complexity and enable volume discounts. Single sourcing is reserved for justified cases such as a development partner or very high tooling costs (metal/plastic casting).

### The combined ABC/XYZ analysis guides the supply strategy. Which statements are correct? (Mehrfachauswahl)
- [x] ABC classifies parts by quantity-value ratio (A = expensive parts needed in small quantities)
- [x] XYZ classifies parts by demand fluctuation (Z = strongly fluctuating demand)
- [x] The three supply principles are stock-keeping, individual procurement as needed, and synchronous delivery (JIT/JIS)
- [ ] JIT delivery increases the required storage space in the factory layout
> Explanation: ABC ranks by value/quantity, XYZ by demand variability. Supply principles: inventory holding (stock-triggered), individual procurement as needed (demand-triggered), and synchronous JIT/JIS delivery (time-triggered). JIT eliminates storage space but needs more traffic area — an important effect on structure and layout planning.

### The SCOR model is a key process reference model in SCM. What are its core processes?
- [x] Source, Make, Deliver, Return and Plan — described over four levels of detail (the fourth, company-specific, not part of the basic model)
- [ ] Supply, Manufacture, Distribute — over two levels
- [ ] Sense, Model, Decide, Route, Ship — over three levels
- [ ] Store, Move, Deliver, Recycle, Plan — over five levels
> Explanation: The SCOR model (Supply Chain Operations Reference, by the Supply Chain Council) rests on five processes — Source, Make, Deliver, Return, Plan — across four levels: level 1 top/scope, level 2 configuration (16 categories, e.g. M1 make-to-stock), level 3 design (sub-processes), level 4 implementation (company-specific, not in the basic model).

### According to the lecture, which "soft factors" form the foundation of a functioning (win-win) Supply Chain Management?
- [x] Trust, responsibility and transparency
- [ ] Price, power and penalties
- [ ] Automation, digitalization and robotics
- [ ] Speed, scale and standardization
> Explanation: Functioning SCM is a lived philosophy resting on trust (granting suppliers autonomy for the chain's benefit), responsibility (misconduct breaks trust and harms the win-win), and transparency (each partner discloses material/information requirements via process-oriented, cross-company information systems with clearly defined interfaces).

### Match warehouse type to purpose. Which is correct?
- [x] Buffer warehouses compensate short-term demand fluctuations (e.g. between manufacturing and assembly); inventory warehouses balance medium/long-term fluctuations; distribution warehouses also perform picking (changing the load-unit composition)
- [ ] Buffer warehouses balance long-term fluctuations; inventory warehouses handle only picking
- [ ] Distribution warehouses never change the composition of load units
- [ ] All three types serve exactly the same function
> Explanation: By purpose within the material flow: inventory (Vorrats) warehouses balance medium/long-term demand fluctuations and secure delivery readiness; buffer warehouses absorb short-term fluctuations between stations; distribution warehouses additionally pick, altering the load unit between receipt and dispatch.

### Why does dynamic (chaotic/free) storage-location management usually need less space than fixed storage-location management?
- [x] Because items are distributed arbitrarily across locations, so with stochastically independent inventory trends high and low stocks of different items balance out
- [ ] Because each item is permanently assigned a location that is never shared
- [ ] Because it stores several load units per location
- [ ] Because it ignores warehouse receptiveness entirely
> Explanation: With fixed assignment, the free capacity of one item's reserved locations cannot be used by others, and the warehouse is "full" as soon as one item has no free slot. With dynamic/chaotic management, independent inventory fluctuations of different articles offset each other, so less total space is required for the same availability.

### A warehouse starts a period with b(t₀) = 120 units. During the period the inflow is z(t) = 80 units and the outflow is a(t) = 150 units. What is the inventory b(t) at the end?
- [x] 50 units (120 + 80 − 150)
- [ ] 190 units (120 + 80 − 10)
- [ ] 350 units (120 + 80 + 150)
- [ ] −30 units
> Explanation: The warehouse inventory follows b(t) = b(t₀) + z(t) − a(t) = 120 + 80 − 150 = 50 units. Inflow and outflow are stochastic processes, so the inventory trend is itself stochastic and the warehouse is dimensioned to stay receptive even under unfavourable inflow/outflow patterns.

### In an M/M/1 buffer model, conveyor units arrive at λ = 6/h and are served at µ = 8/h. What is the utilization ρ?
- [x] ρ = 0.75 (λ/µ = 6/8)
- [ ] ρ = 1.33 (µ/λ)
- [ ] ρ = 2 (µ − λ)
- [ ] ρ = 48 (λ·µ)
> Explanation: Utilization ρ = λ/µ = 6/8 = 0.75. In the M/M/1 model (single server, exponentially distributed inter-arrival and service times / Poisson arrivals), ρ must stay below 1 for a stable system; as ρ → 1 the queue length and waiting times grow rapidly.

### In an M/M/1 buffer model with arrival rate λ = 4/h and service rate µ = 5/h, what is the average number of conveyor units in the system, N_S = λ/(µ − λ)?
- [x] N_S = 4 units (4 / (5 − 4))
- [ ] N_S = 0.8 units (λ/µ)
- [ ] N_S = 1 unit (µ − λ)
- [ ] N_S = 20 units (λ·µ)
> Explanation: N_S = λ/(µ − λ) = 4/(5 − 4) = 4 units. Note the small "margin" µ − λ = 1: the closer λ approaches µ, the sharply larger N_S becomes. The average number waiting is N_W = N_S − ρ = 4 − 0.8 = 3.2, and waiting time follows from t_S = N_S/λ.

### How do the two order-picking principles differ?
- [x] Picker-to-part: the picker moves through the warehouse to the items (worst case: traverse the whole warehouse). Part-to-picker: the items are brought to a stationary picking station
- [ ] Picker-to-part: items move to the picker; part-to-picker: the picker moves to the items
- [ ] Both principles require the picker to walk the entire warehouse
- [ ] The two terms describe transport conveyors, not picking
> Explanation: In picker-to-part the picker travels to the goods (potentially traversing the whole warehouse); in part-to-picker the goods are transported to the picker on storage aids. Additionally, single-stage picking handles orders one by one, while multi-stage picking batches orders (pick combined, then distribute to individual orders).

### How do the push and pull control strategies for intra-company logistics differ?
- [x] Push = central control forecasting production quantities (MRP/MRP II/OPT), tending to high inventories and long lead times; Pull = decentralized, consumer-triggered control (Kanban) with low inventories and short lead times but poor adaptability to large order fluctuations
- [ ] Push is demand-driven with low inventories; pull is forecast-driven with high inventories
- [ ] Push uses Kanban; pull uses MRP II
- [ ] Both strategies are identical and use the same concepts
> Explanation: In the push strategy a central unit pushes orders through production per MRP/MRP II/OPT — enabling cost-minimizing coordination and forecast adjustment but causing high inventory, capital commitment and long throughput. In the pull strategy (Kanban, from the Toyota Production System) each station is replenished by its consumer, giving high delivery service at low inventory and low control effort, but working best for low-complexity, flow-oriented production with low set-up times.
