# Derivatives · VL 05 – Swaps

### What distinguishes a swap from futures and options as a class of derivatives?
- [ ] Swaps are always exchange traded and centrally cleared
- [x] In a swap pre-determined cash flows are exchanged against each other at multiple dates, instead of a cash flow against an underlying
- [ ] Swaps always require an upfront premium like options
- [ ] Swaps have exactly one payment date at maturity
> Explanation: In futures/options a cash flow is exchanged against an underlying; in a swap, cash flows are exchanged against each other at multiple dates according to pre-agreed rules. Swaps are mostly traded OTC, and no premium is paid at inception – the contract starts with zero value.

### In a plain vanilla interest rate swap with notional €100mn, the fixed rate is 3.0% (semiannual compounding, paid semiannually). The 6-month LIBOR fixed six months ago was 2.4%. What is the net payment of the fixed rate payer at the current payment date?
- [x] He pays €0.3mn net
- [ ] He receives €0.3mn net
- [ ] He pays €0.6mn net
- [ ] He pays €1.5mn net
> Explanation: Fixed leg: 100mn × 3%/2 = 1.5mn. Floating leg: 100mn × 2.4%/2 = 1.2mn (the floating payment uses the LIBOR fixed at the start of the period). Net: pays 1.5 − 1.2 = 0.3mn. Notionals are not exchanged in an interest rate swap.

### Company A can borrow fixed at 4.0% or floating at LIBOR+0.3%. Company B can borrow fixed at 5.2% or floating at LIBOR+0.9%. What is the total borrowing cost saving a swap can generate (comparative advantage)?
- [ ] 1.2% p.a.
- [x] 0.6% p.a.
- [ ] 0.3% p.a.
- [ ] 0% – swaps only save transaction costs
> Explanation: Fixed-rate difference 5.2% − 4.0% = 1.2%, floating-rate difference 0.9% − 0.3% = 0.6%. The total gain equals the difference of these spreads: 1.2% − 0.6% = 0.6%, to be split between A, B (and possibly an intermediary). If the spreads were equal, there would be no comparative advantage and the swap could only save transaction costs.

### Why does a financial intermediary (FI) typically appear in swaps, and what is the consequence for the counterparties?
- [x] Companies usually do not negotiate directly; the FI acts as market maker and earns a few basis points, making conditions slightly worse for both counterparties
- [ ] Regulation forbids direct swaps between non-banks
- [ ] The FI guarantees both legs free of charge
- [ ] The FI removes the need for a notional
> Explanation: In practice counterparties deal with a bank acting as market maker. The FI's margin (a few bp) makes the effective rates for both companies slightly worse than in a direct swap.

### From the perspective of the floating rate payer, how can a plain vanilla interest rate swap be valued as a bond portfolio?
- [x] VSwap = Bfix − Bfloat, where Bfloat = (L + k*)·e^(−r*t*) with the next floating payment k* at time t*
- [ ] VSwap = Bfloat − Bfix, where Bfloat equals the notional at any point in time
- [ ] VSwap = Bfix − L, since the floating leg has no value
- [ ] VSwap = (Bfix − Bfloat)·e^(−rT), discounted to maturity
> Explanation: The floating rate payer receives the fixed leg (long fixed bond) and pays the floating leg (short floater). The floater is worth its face value immediately after each reset, so today Bfloat = (L + k*)e^(−r*t*). Swap rates (swap zero curve) are used for discounting to account for bank credit risk.

### What is the alternative to valuing an interest rate swap as two bonds, and why do both approaches give the same value?
- [ ] Valuing it as an option portfolio using Black/Scholes
- [x] Valuing it as a portfolio of FRAs: expected floating payments are computed from forward rates and all net cash flows discounted; forward rates are consistent with the same zero curve
- [ ] Simulating future LIBOR paths with Monte Carlo
- [ ] Using the duration of both legs
> Explanation: The expected floating payments can be replaced by the forward rates derived from the current term structure. Discounting the expected net cash flows yields exactly the same value as the bond approach, since both rest on the same no-arbitrage zero curve.

### Which feature distinguishes a currency swap from an interest rate swap?
- [ ] Currency swaps have no fixed leg
- [x] In a currency swap the principals are exchanged at the beginning and at the end of the swap
- [ ] Currency swaps are exchange traded
- [ ] Interest payments in a currency swap are netted against each other
> Explanation: In an interest rate swap notionals are not exchanged (same currency, would cancel). In a currency swap principal and interest payments are in different currencies, and the principals are exchanged at the start and at maturity. Payments are not netted since they are in different currencies.

### From the perspective of the foreign currency payer, the value of a currency swap (in domestic currency, indirect quotation S₀) is …
- [x] VSwap = Bdom − Bfor/S₀
- [ ] VSwap = Bdom − Bfor·S₀
- [ ] VSwap = Bfor − Bdom/S₀
- [ ] VSwap = (Bdom − Bfor)/S₀
> Explanation: The foreign currency payer receives the domestic payments (long domestic bond) and pays the foreign ones (short foreign bond). The foreign bond value is translated at S₀ (foreign currency units per unit of domestic currency, indirect quotation). Alternatively the swap can be valued as a portfolio of currency forwards.

### In a CDS, which obligations do the two counterparties have?
- [x] The protection buyer pays a periodic spread; the protection seller compensates the notional in case of a credit event, typically against delivery of the bond
- [ ] The protection seller pays a periodic spread; the buyer delivers the bond at maturity
- [ ] Both parties exchange fixed against floating interest payments on the reference bond
- [ ] The protection buyer receives the bond coupons from the seller
> Explanation: The buyer of protection pays the CDS spread (e.g. 120 bp p.a. on the notional). On default of the reference entity the seller pays the notional, and with physical settlement the buyer delivers the bond; with a mid-year default a pro-rata accrual payment is due. The exact definition of the credit event is fundamental.

### A CDS trades at a spread of 180 bp; the assumed recovery rate is 40%. What is the implied risk-neutral one-year default probability (Duffie/Singleton)?
- [ ] 1.80%
- [x] 2.96%
- [ ] 4.50%
- [ ] 1.08%
> Explanation: λ = s/LGD = 0.018/0.6 = 0.03; p = 1 − e^(−λ·1) = 1 − e^(−0.03) = 2.96%. With 1.80% the recovery rate was ignored, with 4.50%... s/RR was used, with 1.08% s·LGD. Note: this is a risk-neutral, not the physical default probability.

### Why does the approximation CDS spread ≈ par yield of corporate bond − par yield of government bond often fail in practice?
- [ ] Because CDS markets have no observable prices
- [x] Because bond prices are also driven by liquidity issues and other frictions (e.g. taxes), not only by default risk
- [ ] Because government bonds have higher default risk than CDS
- [ ] Because the recovery rate is always exactly 40%
> Explanation: In theory bond + CDS is riskless, so s = yparB − yparGB. In reality bond prices reflect liquidity premia and other frictions, so the basis deviates. The standard valuation is the Hull/White model: set the spread so that the expected PVs of both counterparties' payments are equal, using risk-neutral probabilities and risk-free discounting.

### An iTraxx Europe contract has a coupon of 100 bp; the quoted spread is 80 bp. Which statement is correct?
- [x] The index price is above 100 and the protection buyer receives an upfront payment when entering
- [ ] The index price is below 100 and the protection buyer must pay upfront
- [ ] Price and coupon are unrelated
- [ ] The protection seller compensates the difference at maturity
> Explanation: (s − c)·rPV = 100 − I. With the quoted spread below the coupon the buyer would pay too much, so I > 100 and the buyer receives (I−100) bp of the nominal upfront. With a credit event in the 125-name index, 0.8% of the notional is settled and the notional is reduced accordingly.

### Why is the quoted spread of a CDS index slightly below the simple average of the constituents' CDS spreads?
- [x] High-spread entities have shorter expected lifetimes; weighting spreads by the risky present value (rPV) of the payments accounts for this
- [ ] Because the index provider deducts a management fee
- [ ] Because defaults are removed from the index retroactively
- [ ] It is not – the quoted spread is exactly the arithmetic average
> Explanation: Entities with very high spreads are expected to pay premiums for a shorter time. The intrinsic index spread therefore weights single-name spreads with their risky PVs: s_index = Σ si·rPVi / Σ rPVi, which lies below the arithmetic average.

### In a total return swap on a bond, what position has the total return payer effectively taken?
- [ ] A leveraged long position in the bond
- [x] A short position in the bond – he profits from any deterioration in its credit quality
- [ ] A riskless money market position
- [ ] A long position in the bond's coupon stream only
> Explanation: The TRS payer passes on coupons and the change in the bond's value and receives LIBOR (plus spread). Economically he has sold the bond short and benefits if the bond loses value. TRS are also used e.g. by non-physically replicating ETFs.

### Which statements about securitization structures are correct? (multiple answers)
- [x] In a CDO the underlying instruments are securities, not loans
- [x] CLOs use bank loans, CMOs use mortgage loans as underlyings
- [x] These structures bear risk because liquid securities are created out of illiquid underlyings and risk assessment is complex
- [ ] In a synthetic CDO the structure is built with long CDS positions
> Explanation: CDO = securities as underlying (vs. ABS with loans); CLO = bank loans, CMO = mortgages, CDO squared = CDOs of CDOs. Risks: liquidity transformation, complex risk assessment (sub-prime crisis), possible maturity transformation by originators. Synthetic CDOs are formed with short (protection-selling) CDS positions, not long ones.
