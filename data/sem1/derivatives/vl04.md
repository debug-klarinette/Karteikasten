# Derivatives · VL 04 – Futures Pricing

### Why is the distinction between investment and consumption assets important for forward pricing?
- [x] For investment assets the forward price follows from no-arbitrage arguments, while for consumption assets arbitrage is restricted (e.g. short selling often impossible)
- [ ] Consumption assets cannot be traded via futures
- [ ] Investment assets have no storage costs, consumption assets always do
- [ ] Forward prices of consumption assets are always below the spot price
> Explanation: Investment assets (stocks, bonds, gold) are held by many investors purely for investment, so carry arbitrage pins down the forward price exactly. For consumption assets (oil, copper) short selling may be impossible and ownership can carry benefits, so the no-arbitrage relation only holds as an inequality, captured by the convenience yield.

### A performance index stands at 15,000. The continuously compounded interest rate is 4%, the future matures in 6 months. What is the arbitrage-free futures price?
- [ ] 15,300.00
- [x] 15,303.02
- [ ] 15,612.18
- [ ] 14,702.99
> Explanation: No income on the underlying: F₀ = S₀·e^(rT) = 15,000·e^(0.04·0.5) = 15,000·e^0.02 = 15,303.02. 15,300 uses simple instead of continuous compounding, 15,612 uses T = 1, 14,703 discounts instead of compounding.

### In the example above the future actually trades at 15,400. How is the arbitrage profit locked in?
- [x] Sell the future and buy the index with borrowed money (cash and carry)
- [ ] Buy the future and buy the index with borrowed money
- [ ] Sell the future and short the index, investing the proceeds
- [ ] Buy the future and short the index, investing the proceeds at the risk-free rate – this is a reverse cash and carry
> Explanation: The future is overpriced (15,400 > 15,303.02). Sell the expensive future, buy the index financed by a loan; at maturity deliver the index and receive 15,400 while repaying 15,303.02 – riskless profit 96.98. The reverse strategy applies when the future is underpriced.

### A stock trades at S₀ = 100 and pays a dividend of 2 in six months. The continuously compounded rate is 4%, the future matures in one year. What is the arbitrage-free futures price?
- [ ] 104.08
- [x] 102.04
- [ ] 100.00
- [ ] 98.04
> Explanation: F₀ = (S₀ − PV(D))·e^(rT) = (100 − 2e^(−0.02))·e^0.04 = (100 − 1.9604)·1.04081 = 102.04. With 104.08 the dividend was ignored. Intermediate income lowers the futures price because the carry-arbitrageur receives the dividend.

### What are the cost of carry, and what determines whether the futures term structure is in contango or backwardation for an asset with continuous yield q?
- [x] Cost of carry = r − q; for r > q futures prices increase with maturity (contango), for r < q they decrease (backwardation)
- [ ] Cost of carry = r + q; the term structure is always in contango
- [ ] Cost of carry = q − r; for q > r futures prices increase with maturity
- [ ] Cost of carry = r·q; the sign has no effect on the term structure
> Explanation: F₀ = S₀·e^((r−q)T). The net cost per dollar in the arbitrage strategy is r − q, which can have either sign – hence futures prices can rise (contango) or fall (backwardation) in maturity.

### What is the purpose of the conversion factor (CF) in the BUND future?
- [ ] It converts the futures price from percent into euros
- [x] It adjusts the delivery payment for differences in coupon and maturity between the deliverable bonds and the hypothetical 6% bond
- [ ] It accounts for accrued interest since the last coupon date
- [ ] It converts continuous into discrete compounding
> Explanation: The FGBL underlying is a hypothetical German government bond with a 6% coupon and 8.5–10.5y maturity. Since actual deliverable bonds differ in coupon and maturity, the payment at delivery (final settlement price × 1,000 × CF) is scaled by the conversion factor to make the deliverable bonds comparable.

### A bank is short the BUND future at delivery. Several bonds are deliverable. Which bond will it deliver?
- [ ] The bond with the highest conversion factor
- [ ] The bond with the longest maturity
- [x] The cheapest-to-deliver bond, i.e. the one with the highest profit (lowest loss) of delivery: counterparty payment DSP·CF minus the bond's market price
- [ ] The bond with the highest coupon
> Explanation: The short position chooses the delivery alternative. It compares for each bond the payment received (DSP × CF, plus accrued interest) with the cost of buying the bond, and delivers the cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) bond.

### The EUR/USD spot rate (indirect quotation) is 1.1000. The continuously compounded EUR rate is 2%, the USD rate 4%. What is the arbitrage-free 6-month forward exchange rate?
- [ ] 1.0891
- [x] 1.1111
- [ ] 1.1222
- [ ] 1.1000
> Explanation: With indirect quotation F₀ = S₀·e^((rf−r)T) = 1.10·e^((0.04−0.02)·0.5) = 1.10·e^0.01 = 1.1111. With 1.0891 the sign of the interest differential is flipped, with 1.1222 T = 1 was used. Because rf > r, the forward rate lies above the spot rate.

### Crude oil trades at S₀ = 80 USD, the interest rate is 3%, storage cost 4% p.a. (both continuous). The 6-month future trades at 81.50 USD. What convenience yield is implied?
- [ ] 7.00%
- [x] 3.28%
- [ ] 1.64%
- [ ] −3.28%
> Explanation: F₀ = S₀·e^((r+u−y)T) ⟹ y = r + u − (1/T)·ln(F₀/S₀) = 0.03 + 0.04 − 2·ln(81.5/80) = 0.07 − 2·0.01858 = 0.0328. The convenience yield captures benefits of physical ownership and limits to arbitrage (no short selling, storage constraints).

### Why can the relation F₀ = S₀·e^((r+u)T) be violated for commodities without creating exploitable arbitrage? (multiple answers)
- [x] Short selling of commodities is often not possible
- [x] Some commodities cannot be stored, or storage is extremely expensive
- [x] Physical ownership of the commodity can provide additional benefits
- [ ] Commodity futures are not legally binding
> Explanation: If the future is too low one would need to short the commodity – often impossible. Storage may be unavailable (electric power) and ownership conveys benefits. All of this is summarized in the convenience yield y: F₀ = S₀·e^((r+u−y)T).

### When are futures prices higher than forward prices on the same underlying?
- [ ] Always, because futures have daily settlement
- [ ] When interest rates are constant over time
- [x] When the spot price and the interest rate are positively correlated
- [ ] When the underlying pays no income
> Explanation: With positive correlation, margin gains arrive when rates are high (reinvested attractively) and losses when rates are low (cheap to finance) – an advantage for the future, so F > forward price. With zero correlation (or constant rates) the expected NPV of margining cash flows is zero and both prices coincide; with negative correlation the forward is more expensive.

### A bank holds a long forward contract entered some time ago at F₀ = 50. Today the forward price for the same maturity is Ft = 54, the continuously compounded rate is 5% and the remaining maturity 0.5 years. What is the value of the position?
- [ ] 4.00
- [x] 3.90
- [ ] 4.10
- [ ] 0
> Explanation: ft = (Ft − F₀)·e^(−r(T−t)) = 4·e^(−0.025) = 3.90. The price difference is realized only at maturity and must therefore be discounted. 4.00 forgets discounting; the value at inception (not now) was zero.
