# Derivatives · VL 03 – Interest Rates

### A nominal interest rate of 8% p.a. is compounded quarterly. What is the effective annual rate?
- [ ] 8.00%
- [x] 8.24%
- [ ] 8.33%
- [ ] 8.16%
> Explanation: (1 + 0.08/4)⁴ − 1 = 1.02⁴ − 1 = 8.243%. 8.33% would be the continuous limit (e^0.08 − 1), 8.16% corresponds to semiannual compounding.

### What happens to the effective annual rate as the compounding period becomes infinitesimally small?
- [ ] It converges to the nominal rate r
- [x] It converges to e^r − 1, the continuously compounded equivalent
- [ ] It grows without bound
- [ ] It converges to r·ln(2)
> Explanation: lim (1 + r/m)^m = e^r as m → ∞. The continuously compounded rate is the limit of ever more frequent compounding; it does not explode but converges.

### What is the n-year zero (spot) rate?
- [x] The effective yearly rate on a riskless investment starting today, lasting exactly n years, without intermediate payments
- [ ] The coupon rate that makes a bond trade at par
- [ ] The average of the coupon rates of all n-year government bonds
- [ ] The rate agreed today for an investment starting in n years
> Explanation: The n-year zero rate corresponds to a Treasury zero bond held for n years – no intermediate payments. The coupon rate equalizing price and face value is the par yield; a rate agreed today for a future period is a forward rate.

### Why should swap zero rates normally be slightly higher than government zero rates, and what is the difference called?
- [x] Swap rates reflect the higher credit risk of banks compared to the government; the difference is the swap spread
- [ ] Swap rates include compensation for inflation; the difference is the real rate
- [ ] Swap markets are less liquid; the difference is the liquidity premium of Treasuries
- [ ] They should be lower, because swaps are collateralized; the difference is the repo spread
> Explanation: Swap zero rates are derived from interbank interest rate swaps. Banks carry more credit risk than the government, so swap rates should exceed Treasury rates – the swap spread. Notably, the 30y swap spread turned negative after the financial crisis, possibly due to duration-hedging demand from pension funds and life insurers.

### Why is LIBOR being replaced as a money market benchmark rate?
- [ ] The ECB decided to centralize all benchmark fixings
- [ ] The underlying interbank market became too large to survey
- [x] Rigging of the survey-based fixing was detected after the financial crisis
- [ ] EURIBOR proved more accurate for all five LIBOR currencies
> Explanation: LIBOR was fixed by surveying panel banks, and manipulation (LIBOR rigging) was detected after the financial crisis. It was ceased by end 2021/2023 and replaced by transaction-based risk-free rates (RFRs); the EURIBOR (hybrid method) continues to be published.

### How is the theoretical price of a coupon bond calculated?
- [ ] All cash flows are discounted at the bond's coupon rate
- [ ] All cash flows are discounted at the current 1-year rate
- [x] Each cash flow (coupons and principal) is discounted at the zero rate matching its payment date
- [ ] The face value is discounted at the YTM and coupons are added undiscounted
> Explanation: Theoretical pricing requires the zero rates for all relevant maturities; each cash flow is discounted at "its" zero rate. The YTM is then the single discount rate making the present value equal to the market price – it is an output of the price, not the input for theoretical pricing.

### A bond has a Macaulay duration of 7 (continuous compounding). The interest rate rises by 50 basis points. What is the approximate relative price change?
- [ ] −7.0%
- [x] −3.5%
- [ ] +3.5%
- [ ] −0.35%
> Explanation: ΔB/B ≈ −D·Δ = −7 × 0.005 = −3.5%. The price falls when rates rise. −7% would correspond to a 100 bp move, −0.35% to a scaling error.

### What is the Macaulay duration of a zero-coupon bond?
- [x] Its time to maturity
- [ ] Zero
- [ ] Half its time to maturity
- [ ] Its YTM divided by its maturity
> Explanation: Duration is the present-value-weighted average time of the bond's payments. A zero bond has only one payment at maturity, so D equals the maturity.

### When must the modified duration D* = D/(1 + r/m) be used instead of the Macaulay duration?
- [ ] When the bond pays no coupons
- [x] When the interest rate is expressed with a compounding frequency of m times per year instead of continuous compounding
- [ ] When the term structure is not flat
- [ ] When the bond is callable
> Explanation: ΔB/B = −D·Δ holds for continuously compounded rates. For rates with discrete compounding (m per year) the sensitivity is ΔB/B = −D/(1 + r/m)·Δ = −D*·Δ.

### Why does the duration approximation systematically misestimate bond price changes for large interest rate moves, and how can it be improved?
- [ ] It overestimates price gains; improvement requires a higher discount rate
- [x] It is a linear approximation to a convex price curve; adding the convexity term ΔB/B ≈ −D·Δ + ½·C·Δ² improves it
- [ ] It ignores coupon payments; improvement requires using the YTM
- [ ] It assumes a flat term structure; improvement requires the Svensson method
> Explanation: Duration is the tangent (first-order Taylor term) to the hyperbolic price function. Because the true curve is convex, duration underestimates price increases and overestimates decreases. The second-order term, the convexity C, corrects this: ΔB/B ≈ −D·Δ + ½CΔ².

### Continuously compounded spot rates are r₂ = 2.0% and r₃ = 2.5%. What is the forward rate f(2,3)?
- [ ] 2.25%
- [ ] 3.0%
- [x] 3.5%
- [ ] 4.5%
> Explanation: f(t−1,t) = t·rt − (t−1)·rt−1 = 3 × 2.5% − 2 × 2.0% = 7.5% − 4.0% = 3.5%. For a normal (upward sloping) term structure, forward rates lie above spot rates. 2.25% is the simple average – wrong direction of reasoning.

### How can the t-year continuously compounded spot rate be interpreted in terms of forward rates?
- [x] As the average of the t one-year forward rates starting in years 0 to t−1
- [ ] As the maximum of the one-year forward rates
- [ ] As the last one-year forward rate before t
- [ ] As the product of the one-year forward rates
> Explanation: From e^(t·rt) = Π e^(f) it follows rt = (1/t)·Σ f(k−1,k). With continuous compounding the spot rate is exactly the arithmetic average of the one-year forward rates.

### Which theory of the term structure argues that investors demand higher returns for longer maturities because they want to stay liquid?
- [ ] Expectations theory
- [x] Liquidity preference theory
- [ ] Market segmentation theory
- [ ] Ultimate forward rate theory
> Explanation: Liquidity preference: investors accept long-term investments only at higher returns. Expectations theory says forward rates equal expected future zero rates (in a sense tautological). Market segmentation says short, medium and long rates are determined independently – consistent with the observation that the very long end can be downward sloping.

### What is the purpose of the EIOPA ultimate forward rate (UFR)?
- [ ] It caps the rates banks may use for discounting deposits
- [ ] It is the highest forward rate observed in the Svensson estimation
- [x] It provides a theoretical anchor (expected long-term real growth plus inflation) to which the term structure converges beyond the last liquid point, because Svensson extrapolation beyond 30 years can produce strange patterns
- [ ] It is the floor for negative interest rates
> Explanation: Applying the Svensson method beyond observable maturities can produce strange term structure patterns. EIOPA therefore sets a UFR (e.g. 3.6% = long-term real growth + inflation) and assumes the curve converges from the last liquid point (year 20) to the UFR by year 60.

### Which statements about the term structure of interest rates are correct? (multiple answers)
- [x] A normal term structure is upward sloping, a downward sloping one is called inverse
- [x] In practice the spot curve is estimated with the Svensson method (used by Bundesbank, ECB, FED)
- [x] For a normal term structure, forward rates lie above the corresponding spot rates
- [ ] The term structure shows the relationship between coupon rates and bond ratings
> Explanation: The term structure relates zero bond maturities to their yields. Normal = upward sloping, inverse = downward sloping. Central banks estimate it with the Svensson method and publish the parameters daily. Since the spot rate is the average of the forward rates, forwards lie above spots when the curve rises – not below.
