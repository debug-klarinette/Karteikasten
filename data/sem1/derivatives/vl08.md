# Derivatives · VL 08 – Implied Volatility & the Greeks

### What is the implied volatility of an option?
- [x] The volatility for which the Black/Scholes price equals the observed market price
- [ ] The standard deviation of the stock's historical log returns
- [ ] The volatility forecast published by the exchange
- [ ] The realized volatility until the option's maturity
> Explanation: IV is backed out from market prices via the B/S model (e.g. by numerical iteration). It can differ from historical volatility because market expectations differ from the past or because the B/S model is not perfectly applicable. Traders often quote IVs instead of option prices.

### An at-the-money-forward call (S = K·e^(−rT)) on a stock with S = 100 and T = 1 trades at C = 4. What is the approximate implied volatility (Brenner/Subrahmanyam)?
- [ ] 4%
- [x] 10%
- [ ] 16%
- [ ] 25%
> Explanation: For S = Ke^(−rT): σ ≈ (C/S)·√(2π/T) = 0.04·2.507 = 10.0%. The rule of thumb is σ ≈ 2.5·C/(S√T). Corrado/Miller provide a more general approximation valid within ±10% of the discounted strike.

### Which statements about the VIX are correct? (multiple answers)
- [x] It is a model-free implied volatility measure, not based on the B/S approximations
- [x] It is based on S&P 500 options with maturities between 23 and 37 days, i.e. measures one-month IV
- [x] It uses the bid-ask midpoints of out-of-the-money calls and puts
- [ ] It measures the realized volatility of the S&P 500 over the last month
> Explanation: The VIX (CBOE, calculated in one-minute intervals) aggregates OTM option quotes Q(Ki) in a model-free formula – it is forward looking. Empirically, implied volatility exceeds realized volatility most of the time; this volatility premium is around 3–4 percentage points.

### What is the volatility skew observed in index option markets?
- [x] Implied volatility tends to be lower for options with higher strikes
- [ ] Implied volatility is identical across all strikes, as B/S assumes
- [ ] Implied volatility rises linearly with time to maturity
- [ ] Implied volatility is always higher for calls than for puts
> Explanation: The IV surface shows lower IVs for higher strikes (skew); sometimes IV picks up again for very high strikes ("smile"). The maturity dimension shows no clear pattern. This contradicts the constant-σ assumption of Black/Scholes.

### What is the delta of an option?
- [x] The change in the option price for a small change in the price of the underlying – the slope of the tangent to the pricing function; for a call Δ = N(d₁)
- [ ] The change in the option price as one day of maturity passes
- [ ] The ratio of option price to stock price
- [ ] The probability that the option expires worthless
> Explanation: Δ = ∂c/∂S = N(d₁) for a call (put: N(d₁) − 1 < 0). It is a linear approximation of the option value function and corresponds to the hedge ratio from the B/S derivation and the binomial model.

### A trader has sold 2,000 calls (one share each) with Δ = 0.6. How does he delta-hedge the position?
- [x] Buy 1,200 shares
- [ ] Sell 1,200 shares
- [ ] Buy 800 shares
- [ ] Buy 2,000 shares
> Explanation: The short call position has delta −2,000 × 0.6 = −1,200; buying 1,200 shares sets the portfolio delta to zero. Note: delta changes with S and t, so the hedge must be adjusted dynamically (and is also affected by changes in volatility and maturity).

### How does the delta of a call behave?
- [x] It rises from near 0 (deep out of the money) to near 1 (deep in the money) and is roughly 0.5 at the money
- [ ] It is constant at 0.5 for all stock prices
- [ ] It falls from 1 to 0 as the stock price rises
- [ ] It is negative for low stock prices
> Explanation: Δcall = N(d₁) ∈ (0,1): deep OTM calls barely react to the stock (Δ ≈ 0), deep ITM calls move one-for-one (Δ ≈ 1). Put deltas are negative, between −1 and 0.

### Which Greeks of a long call on a non-income paying stock are positive? (multiple answers)
- [x] Delta
- [x] Vega
- [x] Rho
- [ ] Theta
> Explanation: For a call: Δ = N(d₁) > 0, Vega = S₀√T·N′(d₁) > 0, Rho = KTe^(−rT)N(d₂) > 0, Gamma > 0 – but Theta < 0: the option loses value as time passes, for both calls and puts (European, non-income underlying).

### What does gamma measure, and what does a high gamma imply for delta hedging?
- [x] The change of delta with the underlying's price (Γ = N′(d₁)/(S₀σ√T)); a high gamma means the delta hedge must be adjusted frequently
- [ ] The sensitivity to volatility; a high gamma makes the hedge insensitive
- [ ] The time decay of the option; high gamma means slow decay
- [ ] The leverage of the option position
> Explanation: Gamma is the second derivative of the option price with respect to S – the curvature that the linear delta approximation ignores. It is identical for calls and puts and positive for long options. High gamma (ATM, short maturity) means delta changes quickly, requiring frequent rebalancing. Leverage is measured by Omega = Δ·S₀/c.

### A portfolio contains 100 long calls with Δ = 0.5 and 200 short puts with Δ = −0.4 (per option). What is the portfolio delta?
- [ ] −30
- [x] +130
- [ ] +50
- [ ] −130
> Explanation: ΔP = Σ xi·Δi = 100 × 0.5 + (−200) × (−0.4) = 50 + 80 = +130. The portfolio Greek is the weighted sum of the position Greeks; short positions enter with negative quantity, so shorting negative-delta puts adds positive delta.

### What is the delta of a futures contract on an underlying with continuous yield q?
- [ ] Exactly 1, because the future moves one-for-one with the spot
- [x] e^((r−q)T) – close to but not exactly 1; for an existing forward position, however, the delta is exactly 1
- [ ] N(d₁), as for a call
- [ ] Zero, because no premium is paid
> Explanation: F₀ = S₀e^((r−q)T) implies ∂F₀/∂S₀ = e^((r−q)T) ≠ 1 (close to 1 for small cost of carry or short maturity). For an already opened forward, ft = St − F₀e^(−r(T−t)) gives ∂ft/∂St = 1. The difference is caused by the daily marking to market, which applies only to futures.
