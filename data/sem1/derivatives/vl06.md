# Derivatives · VL 06 – Options (1): Payoffs, Strategies & Binomial Model

### How do EUREX stock options typically differ from DAX index options (ODAX)?
- [x] Stock options have physical delivery and American exercise; ODAX is cash settled and European
- [ ] Stock options are European, ODAX is American
- [ ] Stock options are cash settled, ODAX has physical delivery
- [ ] There is no difference except the underlying
> Explanation: ODAX: cash settlement, European exercise, multiplier 5 € per index point. Stock options work similarly, but with physical delivery and American exercise rights.

### Why are strike price and contract multiplier of stock options adjusted in case of capital increases or stock splits?
- [ ] To increase the option's value in line with the new share count
- [x] So that the value of the option remains (almost) unaffected by the corporate action
- [ ] To compensate the option writer for dilution losses
- [ ] Because the exchange must keep all strikes at round numbers
> Explanation: Corporate actions (splits, pre-emptive rights, special dividends) mechanically change the stock price. The R-factor adjustment (multiplier divided by R, strike multiplied by R) keeps the exercise gain and thus the option value essentially unchanged.

### A trader buys a European call with strike K = 50 for a premium of 4. At maturity ST = 57. What is his profit?
- [ ] 7
- [x] 3
- [ ] −4
- [ ] 11
> Explanation: Payoff = max(ST − K, 0) = 7; profit = payoff − premium = 7 − 4 = 3. With 11 the premium was added instead of subtracted; −4 would be the profit for ST ≤ 50.

### A put with strike 60 trades at 7 while the stock trades at 55. How does the option value decompose?
- [x] Intrinsic value 5, time value 2
- [ ] Intrinsic value 7, time value 0
- [ ] Intrinsic value 2, time value 5
- [ ] Intrinsic value 0, time value 7
> Explanation: The put is in the money by K − S = 60 − 55 = 5 (intrinsic value). Time value = option price − intrinsic value = 7 − 5 = 2.

### An investor buys a call and a put with the same maturity and the same strike (at the money). What is this strategy and what is the bet?
- [ ] A strangle – a bet on stable prices
- [x] A straddle – a bet on large price moves in either direction (long volatility)
- [ ] A bullish spread – a bet on rising prices
- [ ] A butterfly spread – a bet on falling volatility
> Explanation: Call + put at the same strike = straddle, profitable for large moves up or down: a long volatility position. A strangle uses different strikes (OTM call and OTM put) and is the cheaper variant.

### How is a bullish spread constructed with calls, and what characterizes its payoff?
- [x] Buy a call at the lower strike KL, sell a call at the higher strike KH; both profit and loss are capped
- [ ] Buy calls at both strikes; profit is unlimited
- [ ] Sell a call at KL, buy a call at KH; profits from falling prices
- [ ] Buy a call and a put at the same strike
> Explanation: Bullish spread: long call KL, short call KH (KL < KH). The net premium paid is the maximum loss; the maximum gain is KH − KL minus net premium. Reversing the positions yields a bearish spread. Spreads are capital protection strategies.

### Which strategy profits when the underlying stays close to its current level (short volatility)? (multiple answers)
- [x] Short straddle
- [x] Long butterfly spread
- [ ] Long straddle
- [x] Short strangle
> Explanation: Selling a straddle or strangle collects premiums that are kept if prices stay put. A long butterfly (buy call KL, sell two calls ATM, buy call KH) also has its maximum payoff at the middle strike. A long straddle is the opposite bet – long volatility.

### A non-dividend stock trades at S₀ = 50; a European call with K = 48, T = 1 and r = 5% (continuous) trades at c = 4.00. Is there an arbitrage opportunity?
- [x] Yes – the lower bound c ≥ S₀ − K·e^(−rT) = 4.34 is violated; buy the call, short the stock, invest the proceeds
- [ ] No – the call price only has to exceed S₀ − K = 2
- [ ] Yes – the call may never cost less than the stock itself
- [ ] No – lower bounds only apply to American options
> Explanation: Lower bound: c ≥ max(S₀ − Ke^(−rT), 0) = 50 − 48·e^(−0.05) = 50 − 45.66 = 4.34 > 4.00. Strategy: buy the underpriced call, sell the share at 50, invest 45.66 at r; the initial cash inflow plus a sure non-negative final payoff is an arbitrage. Note the bound uses the discounted strike, not S₀ − K.

### A stock trades at S₀ = 30, a European call with K = 29, T = 0.25 and r = 6% (continuous) costs c = 2.52. What is the price of the corresponding European put (no dividends)?
- [ ] 1.52
- [x] 1.09
- [ ] 0.48
- [ ] 2.09
> Explanation: Put-call parity: p = c + K·e^(−rT) − S₀ = 2.52 + 29·e^(−0.015) − 30 = 2.52 + 28.57 − 30 = 1.09. With 1.52 the strike was not discounted. The parity follows from the fact that stock + put + short call + loan replicates a riskless zero payoff.

### Why is early exercise of an American call on a non-dividend paying stock never optimal (at positive interest rates)?
- [x] Because c ≥ S₀ − K·e^(−rT) > S₀ − K before maturity: selling the option always dominates exercising it
- [ ] Because American calls cannot be exercised before maturity by contract design
- [ ] Because the exchange charges early exercise fees
- [ ] Because the stock price always rises until maturity
> Explanation: The lower bound implies the option's market value always exceeds its exercise value S₀ − K. Early exercise forgoes interest on the strike without gaining anything (no dividend to capture). Hence American and European calls on non-dividend stocks have the same value. For puts the reasoning fails: early exercise earns interest on the strike, so it can be optimal.

### S₀ = 100, u = 1.2, d = 0.8, r = 5% (continuous), T = 1, one binomial step. What is the price of a European call with K = 100?
- [ ] 12.56
- [x] 11.95
- [ ] 9.51
- [ ] 20.00
> Explanation: p = (e^(rT) − d)/(u − d) = (1.0513 − 0.8)/0.4 = 0.6282. Payoffs: fu = 20, fd = 0. c = e^(−0.05)·(0.6282·20) = 0.9512·12.56 = 11.95. 12.56 forgets discounting, 9.51 uses p = 0.5.

### What is the meaning of the risk-neutral probability p in the binomial model?
- [ ] It is the empirically estimated probability of an upward move
- [x] It is the artificial probability under which the stock's expected return equals the risk-free rate, allowing derivatives to be valued by discounting expected payoffs at r
- [ ] It is the probability that maximizes the option value
- [ ] It is the hedge ratio of the riskless portfolio
> Explanation: Under p the stock is a martingale: E(ST) = S₀e^(rT). Risk-neutral valuation prices the option as the expected present value of its payoff using p and the risk-free discount rate – even though the physical probability q > p reflects risk aversion. The hedge ratio Δ = (fu − fd)/(uS₀ − dS₀) is a separate quantity.

### European put, S₀ = 50, K = 52, u = 1.2, d = 0.8, r = 5% (continuous), two binomial steps of one year (from the lecture: p = 0.6282, value 4.19). What changes if the put is American?
- [x] Early exercise after a down move is optimal (12 > 9.46); the value rises to 5.09
- [ ] Nothing – early exercise is never optimal for puts
- [ ] The value falls, because exercise destroys time value
- [ ] The value doubles, because there are two exercise dates
> Explanation: At the down node the continuation value e^(−0.05)·(0.6282·4 + 0.3718·20) = 9.46 is below the exercise value K − dS₀ = 52 − 40 = 12, so exercising early is optimal. Rolling back with fd = 12 gives 5.09 instead of 4.19; the early exercise premium is 0.90. For American puts the recursive node-by-node check is required.

### The volatility of a stock is σ = 30% p.a. and one binomial step is Δt = 0.25 years. What is the up factor u according to Cox/Ross/Rubinstein?
- [x] u = e^(0.3·√0.25) = 1.1618
- [ ] u = e^(0.3·0.25) = 1.0779
- [ ] u = e^(0.3) = 1.3499
- [ ] u = 1 + 0.3·0.25 = 1.0750
> Explanation: u = e^(σ√Δt) = e^(0.3·0.5) = e^0.15 = 1.1618 (and d = 1/u). The volatility scales with the square root of time (random walk), not linearly. As Δt → 0 the binomial distribution converges to the normal, and binomial prices converge to Black/Scholes values.
