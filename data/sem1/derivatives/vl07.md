# Derivatives · VL 07 – Options (2): Stochastic Processes & Black/Scholes

### A variable z follows a Wiener process with variance rate 1.0 per year. What is the standard deviation of the change z(T) − z(0) over T = 4 years?
- [ ] 4
- [x] 2
- [ ] 1
- [ ] 16
> Explanation: The change over T is the sum of N independent increments ε√Δt, so the variance is N·Δt = T = 4 and the standard deviation √T = 2. Uncertainty grows with the square root of time, not linearly.

### A variable follows the generalized Wiener process dx = 3dt + 4dz. What are mean and standard deviation of the change of x over 4 years?
- [x] Mean 12, standard deviation 8
- [ ] Mean 12, standard deviation 16
- [ ] Mean 3, standard deviation 4
- [ ] Mean 12, standard deviation 4
> Explanation: Mean = aT = 3·4 = 12; standard deviation = b√T = 4·√4 = 8. The variance rate b² scales with T, the standard deviation with √T – 16 would be b·T, a typical error.

### Why is a generalized Wiener process (constant drift rate) inappropriate for stock prices?
- [ ] Because stock prices can become negative under any Wiener process
- [x] Because investors require a constant expected return (percentage drift), not a constant absolute drift – the expected price increase must be proportional to the price level
- [ ] Because stock prices have no random component
- [ ] Because the variance of stock prices is constant over time
> Explanation: The required return is independent of the price level, so the expected drift is μS (not constant a) and the volatility term σS is also proportional to S. This gives dS = μS·dt + σS·dz – Geometric Brownian Motion, the continuous-time analogue of the binomial random walk.

### What is the role of Itô's Lemma in derivatives pricing?
- [x] It gives the stochastic process followed by a function G(S,t) of an Itô process – e.g. the process of a derivative's price as a function of the underlying
- [ ] It proves that stock returns are normally distributed in any model
- [ ] It eliminates the drift from every stochastic process
- [ ] It states that all derivatives earn the risk-free rate
> Explanation: If dS = μSdt + σSdz, Itô's Lemma yields dG = (∂G/∂S·μS + ∂G/∂t + ½·∂²G/∂S²·σ²S²)dt + ∂G/∂S·σS·dz. Crucially, S and G depend on the same source of uncertainty dz – the basis for constructing the riskless hedge portfolio.

### A stock follows GBM with S₀ = 50 and μ = 12%. What is the expected stock price after one year?
- [ ] 56.00
- [x] 56.37
- [ ] 50.00
- [ ] 53.04
> Explanation: Under GBM, ST is lognormally distributed with E(ST) = S₀·e^(μT) = 50·e^0.12 = 56.37. 56.00 uses simple compounding. Note: ln(ST) is normal with mean ln(S₀) + (μ − σ²/2)T, but the expectation of ST itself involves the full μ.

### Which key idea underlies the derivation of the Black-Scholes-Merton differential equation?
- [x] A portfolio of the option and ∂f/∂S shares is instantaneously riskless because both depend on the same source of uncertainty, and must therefore earn the risk-free rate
- [ ] Options are priced by estimating the expected return of the stock μ and discounting at μ
- [ ] The option payoff is replicated by a static buy-and-hold portfolio
- [ ] The stock price follows a binomial distribution in continuous time
> Explanation: Short one derivative, long ∂f/∂S shares: the Wiener term Δz cancels, the portfolio is instantaneously riskless and must earn r. This yields ∂f/∂t + rS·∂f/∂S + ½σ²S²·∂²f/∂S² = rf. The hedge must be rebalanced continuously (dynamic hedging).

### The expected stock return μ does not appear in the Black-Scholes-Merton equation. What does this imply?
- [ ] Option prices are independent of the stock price process
- [x] The equation is independent of risk preferences, so options can be valued in a risk-neutral world (risk-neutral valuation)
- [ ] Options have zero expected return
- [ ] Volatility does not matter for option prices either
> Explanation: Since μ – the only variable affected by risk preferences – drops out, the solution is the same in a risk-neutral world as in the real world. Hence one may assume all assets earn r and discount expected payoffs at r. Volatility σ remains a key input.

### S₀ = K = 50, r = 4%, σ = 30%, T = 1 (no dividends). What is d₁ in the Black/Scholes formula?
- [x] 0.28
- [ ] −0.02
- [ ] 0.13
- [ ] 0.58
> Explanation: d₁ = [ln(S₀/K) + (r + σ²/2)T]/(σ√T) = [0 + (0.04 + 0.045)]/0.3 = 0.085/0.3 = 0.28. −0.02 is d₂ = d₁ − σ√T; 0.13 uses r without the σ²/2 term.

### How are known discrete dividends handled when valuing a European option with Black/Scholes?
- [ ] The strike price is increased by the dividend amount
- [x] The spot price is reduced by the present value of all dividends with ex-dividend date during the option's life
- [ ] The volatility is reduced proportionally to the dividend yield
- [ ] Dividends are ignored for European options
> Explanation: Replace S₀ by S₀ − PV(dividends), discounting from the ex-dividend dates at the risk-free rate. Only dividends with ex-date during the option's life matter – buying on or after the ex-date does not entitle to the dividend. For a continuous yield q, S₀·e^(−qT) is used instead.

### Why can the Black/Scholes formula written in terms of the forward price F₀ be applied to index options regardless of whether the index is a price or a performance (total return) index?
- [x] Because F₀ = S₀·e^((r−q)T) already incorporates whether and which income the underlying pays
- [ ] Because index options never have dividend exposure
- [ ] Because forward prices are independent of interest rates
- [ ] Because performance indexes have zero volatility
> Explanation: Substituting F₀ into Black/Scholes (c = F₀e^(−rT)N(d₁) − Ke^(−rT)N(d₂)) removes the explicit dividend yield: the forward price internalizes the cost of carry. The formula thus applies to income and non-income paying underlyings, e.g. Euro Stoxx (price index) and DAX (performance index) options alike.

### Which model values European currency options, and what role does the foreign interest rate play?
- [x] Garman/Kohlhagen – the foreign interest rate takes the role of a continuous dividend yield of the underlying currency
- [ ] Cox/Ross/Rubinstein – the foreign rate replaces the volatility
- [ ] Black/Scholes without modification – currencies pay no income
- [ ] Hull/White – the foreign rate determines the default probability
> Explanation: A foreign currency holding earns the foreign rate, like a continuous yield q. Garman/Kohlhagen (1983) is Black/Scholes with the foreign interest rate as income yield of the underlying currency.

### Why is there no general closed-form solution for American options, and how are they valued in practice?
- [x] The possibility of early exercise has no analytical solution in general; the binomial model integrates the early exercise check easily
- [ ] Because American options have stochastic volatility by definition
- [ ] Because American options do not satisfy the BSM differential equation
- [ ] They cannot be valued at all and are quoted purely by supply and demand
> Explanation: Early exercise turns valuation into an optimal stopping problem without general analytical solution (approximations exist, e.g. for American calls on dividend stocks). The binomial model handles it by comparing continuation and exercise value at every node.

### Match the exotic option types: which statements are correct? (multiple answers)
- [x] A down-and-out call expires worthless if the underlying falls below a threshold H < S₀
- [x] An Asian option's payoff depends on the average price of the underlying over a period
- [x] A binary option pays a fixed amount M if it ends in the money
- [ ] A lookback option lets the holder choose at half-time whether it is a call or a put
> Explanation: Barrier options depend on price thresholds (down-and-out, up-and-in, ...), Asian options on average prices, binary options pay a fixed amount. The right to choose call or put characterizes a chooser option; a lookback option's payoff depends on the maximum or minimum price during the option's life.
