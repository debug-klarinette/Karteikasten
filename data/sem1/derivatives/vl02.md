# Derivatives · VL 02 – Futures Markets & Hedging

### An investor is long one FDAX contract (tick value €25 per index point). The future was bought at 18,000; the daily settlement price is 18,050. What happens in the margin account?
- [x] The account is credited with €1,250
- [ ] The account is debited with €1,250
- [ ] The account is credited with €50
- [ ] Nothing – gains are only settled at maturity
> Explanation: Marking to market: the long position gains 50 points × €25 = €1,250, which is credited at the end of the trading day. Daily settlement is the key difference between futures and forwards.

### Which margin must be restored when the margin account falls below a critical level?
- [ ] The initial margin is increased by the exchange
- [x] A variation margin payment must bring the account back up once it falls below the maintenance margin
- [ ] The maintenance margin is paid once when the contract is opened
- [ ] No payment is needed as long as the contract has not matured
> Explanation: The initial margin is deposited at opening. If losses push the account below the maintenance margin, the investor must pay a variation margin. Margins minimize the possibility of losses through default.

### What is the most common way a futures position ends?
- [x] Closing out – entering the opposite trade before maturity
- [ ] Physical delivery of the underlying
- [ ] Cash settlement at maturity
- [ ] Default of one counterparty
> Explanation: The vast majority of contracts are closed out: the trader enters the opposite position, and the gain/loss is the change in futures prices. If physical delivery occurs, the short side chooses among delivery alternatives. Cash settlement is used where delivery is inconvenient or impossible (e.g. index futures).

### What is the open interest of a futures contract?
- [ ] The number of trades per day
- [ ] The sum of all long and short positions
- [x] The total number of contracts outstanding, equal to the number of long positions (or equivalently of short positions)
- [ ] The number of contracts that end in physical delivery
> Explanation: Open interest = total number of outstanding contracts. Since each contract has one long and one short, it equals the number of longs or the number of shorts (not their sum). Number of trades per day is the trading volume.

### Futures prices that decrease with maturity are referred to as …
- [ ] Contango
- [x] Backwardation
- [ ] Normal term structure
- [ ] Negative basis
> Explanation: Backwardation = futures prices decline with maturity; contango = they increase. The basis (S − F) is a different concept relating spot and futures prices.

### A hedger goes short a future at F₀ = 100 to hedge a planned sale of the underlying. The hedge is closed before maturity at ST = 90 and FT = 88. What is the effectively received selling price?
- [ ] 90
- [ ] 100
- [x] 102
- [ ] 98
> Explanation: Effective price = ST + (F₀ − FT) = 90 + 12 = 102. Equivalently F₀ + BT with basis BT = ST − FT = 2. Because the basis at closing (2) differs from zero, the result deviates from the locked-in 100 – this is basis risk.

### Why is the hedge riskless when the future is held exactly until its maturity, but not when it is closed earlier?
- [x] At maturity the basis must be zero by arbitrage, while before maturity the basis can deviate from zero in an unpredictable way
- [ ] At maturity the margin account is closed, so no further losses are possible
- [ ] Before maturity futures cannot be sold, only delivered
- [ ] Because the spot price equals the original future price F₀ at maturity
> Explanation: At maturity B = S − F = 0 must hold, otherwise simple arbitrage would be possible. So holding to maturity locks in F₀ exactly. If the contract is closed earlier, the unknown basis BT enters the effective price (F₀ + BT) – the hedge carries basis risk. Note: at maturity ST = FT, not ST = F₀.

### What happens to basis risk when a long-term exposure is hedged by rolling over a series of short-maturity futures?
- [ ] It disappears, because each individual future is held almost to maturity
- [x] It increases with the number of roll-overs, because each roll-over adds a basis change
- [ ] It stays the same as with a single matching future
- [ ] It turns into credit risk
> Explanation: With roll-over hedging the effective price is ST plus the sum of the basis changes at each roll-over date. The more often the future is rolled, the more basis changes accumulate – one reason why long-term hedging is difficult.

### An airline hedges jet fuel purchases with heating oil futures because no jet fuel future exists. What is this called, and how should the contract be chosen?
- [x] Cross hedging; choose the contract whose futures price is most highly correlated with the asset price
- [ ] Natural hedging; choose the contract with the longest maturity
- [ ] Roll-over hedging; choose the contract with the highest trading volume
- [ ] Perfect hedging; choose the contract with the same maturity as the exposure
> Explanation: When the futures underlying differs from the hedged asset, this is cross hedging. Choose the future most highly correlated with the exposure, and a delivery month as close as possible to, but later than, the end of the hedge.

### The standard deviation of spot price changes is σS = 0.02, that of futures price changes σF = 0.03, and the correlation is ρ = 0.9. A position of 600,000 units is hedged; one futures contract covers 15,000 units. What is the optimal number of contracts?
- [ ] 40 contracts
- [x] 24 contracts
- [ ] 54 contracts
- [ ] 27 contracts
> Explanation: h* = ρ·σS/σF = 0.9 × 0.02/0.03 = 0.6. N* = h*·NA/QF = 0.6 × 600,000/15,000 = 24. With 40 you used h = 1, with 54 the inverted ratio ρσF/σS, with 27 you forgot the correlation.

### Why does the optimal hedge ratio h* = ρ·σS/σF minimize the risk of the hedged position?
- [ ] It maximizes the expected profit of the futures position
- [x] It is the value of h for which the derivative of the hedged position's variance σS² + h²σF² − 2hρσSσF with respect to h is zero
- [ ] It guarantees the hedged position has zero variance
- [ ] It equalizes the sizes of spot and futures positions
> Explanation: The variance of the hedged position is σS² + h²σF² − 2hρσSσF. Setting the derivative 2hσF² − 2ρσFσS = 0 yields h* = ρσS/σF. Variance is minimized, but only zero in the special case ρ = 1 and proportional volatilities.

### Which arguments are typically raised against corporate hedging? (multiple answers)
- [x] Hedging is expensive
- [x] Shareholders are usually well diversified and can make their own hedging decisions
- [x] Explaining a loss on the hedge combined with a gain on the underlying can be difficult
- [ ] Hedging makes cash flows more volatile
> Explanation: Cons of hedging: costs, diversified shareholders can hedge themselves, and awkward communication when the hedge loses while the underlying gains. Stabilizing (not destabilizing) cash flows is a pro argument – it lets firms focus on their main business and commit to long-term investments.

### How is the final settlement price of the 3-month EURIBOR future (FEU3) determined?
- [ ] As the volume-weighted average EURIBOR of the delivery month
- [x] As 100 minus the 3-month EURIBOR rate in percent
- [ ] As the 3-month EURIBOR rate in percent
- [ ] As the price of a 3-month zero bond with €1mn face value
> Explanation: Interest rate futures are quoted as 100 − interest rate. A rising interest rate therefore means a falling futures price; the short side profits from rising rates.
